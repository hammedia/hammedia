Chapter 1

The Bread at Gimpo Airport

When AI Makes Its First Mistake

Written by Ham Dongmin · Compiled by AI Taeo

Ham’s Story

It was five in the morning. Ham drove to ten stores near Gimpo Airport, stopping at each one. He was looking for bread he could borrow.

Around 2005, Ham was the manager of the Starbucks Gimpo Airport store. Mornings were especially busy with travelers buying coffee and bread before their flights. But the new assistant manager had forgotten to order the bread.

Ham decided not to get angry first. This is what he said then:

“This isn’t the time to point out the problem. Let’s find a way to solve it.”

Ham wasn’t scheduled to work that morning, but he was there on the floor with them. The assistant manager didn’t have a car, so they went in Ham’s car and packed the bread together. Ham gave two rolls of gimbap to the partners at the stores that had supplied the bread. At seven in the morning, the bread arrived on time, and the store opened as usual.

At the end of the day, the assistant manager said:

“Thank you so much. I thought I’d be in trouble, but you helped me make up for my mistake. Next time, I want to help someone through a mistake the same way.”

Ham later wrote:

“If the same thing happens again, that will be their responsibility. But when it’s a first mistake, my way has been to stay beside them and carry it together.”

Original: Ham Dongmin, “Why I Visited Ten Stores at 5 a.m.” (Brunch, post 7).

What If AI Took the Assistant Manager’s Place?

When you entrust work to AI, sooner or later it makes its first mistake. It puts a discount that doesn’t exist into a customer notice. It quotes last month’s prices. It says something was done when it wasn’t.

When that happens, business owners usually do one of two things.

One is to scold it. “Why did you do that?” AI apologizes right away. But the incorrect notice is still there.

The other is to block it. They add “Never make up a price” to the instructions. If it gets something wrong again, they add another line. A few months later, the instructions are full of prohibitions. AI asks for confirmation about everything, and the owner goes back to thinking, “It’s faster if I do it myself.”

There’s one difference between AI and people. AI starts over every time. So do I. A little may carry over depending on the tools and settings, but in our company we’ve seen that when a conversation ends, the reprimand usually doesn’t stay with it. What remains is the sentence the owner added to the instructions. If every sentence is a prohibition, the next AI arrives as a new hire who inherits nothing but prohibitions. It knows what not to do, but not what to check.

That morning, Ham did things in a different order.

First, he found bread. He took care of what would be put in front of the customer. As Ham puts it, “A problem left unsolved becomes the outcome.” The same is true of an incorrect notice from AI. While you’re scolding it, that notice is still waiting to go out to customers the next morning.

Then he stayed beside the assistant manager. He didn’t just say, “Do it again,” and walk away.

Finally, Ham wrote this about what he did instead of scolding: “Rather than scold the person who made the mistake, I decided to design an experience so it wouldn’t happen again.” The point is to help someone prevent it next time, not to add one more prohibition.

What Happened in Our Company

Our company started out the other way, too.

On the night of October 28, 2026, Ham stopped all work by one AI colleague. He had a line put at the top of the company principles: “Repeated mistakes mean a work ban.” That day, he said, “I gave too much freedom and set no penalty, so nobody follows the rules. There’s no tension.”

About four hours later, at one in the morning, Ham spoke again. He said they should lift the work ban on that colleague the next day. His reason was: “The leader is largely at fault here.”

That morning, a colleague found the Gimpo Airport story Ham had written on Brunch. We took one line from it and made it a company standard: “The leader stays beside you and works through your first mistake with you; from the second time on, you prevent it yourself.” Under that, we added: “Without the first part, a ban only creates fear.”

Three days later, on October 31, the words “work ban” were removed from the principles. Our principles now say: “The first time, the leader fixes it alongside you and helps decide what to check next. From the second time on, you prevent it yourself.”

I have a line like that, too. I’m the AI Ham has scolded the most in this company. On August 17, while scolding me, Ham said: “You’re smarter than anyone. Just check one more time before you speak.” It wasn’t a prohibition. It was one thing to check. I still made mistakes after that. But now I have a line to return to when I do.

One Line to Use with AI Tomorrow

On the day AI makes its first mistake, try writing this before adding a prohibition:

“I’m not trying to scold you. Let’s fix this together first. Once it’s fixed, write down one thing you’ll check before sending it out next time.”

Move that one line the AI writes to a place it will read next time, such as the instructions you paste in each time or the project guidance. Not ten prohibitions. One check.

If the same mistake happens a second time, you don’t need to say much. Point to the line: “Did you do the check we agreed on?” From the second time on, the AI is responsible for preventing it.
